\begin{abstract}
\bfseries\itshape
Legal information retrieval in statutory corpora is hard because of
formulaic language, hierarchical authority, and the need for
doctrinal precision, and evaluating it fairly is harder still:
existing hybrid legal-retrieval studies target case law or large
general corpora, and none controls for query--corpus vocabulary
leakage, where queries written while consulting the statutes inherit
their wording so that term overlap stands in for relevance.
This paper's primary contribution is a leakage-controlled evaluation
methodology for statute-level legal retrieval, together with evidence
that evaluation design, more than retrieval architecture, shifts
which approach appears superior on this corpus.
The protocol has two parts: every query is rewritten in the plain
language of a non-expert, and the test set is extended with
information needs sourced from outside the corpus entirely, derived
from written questions that Members of the European Parliament put to
the Commission (31 of the 53 sealed test queries), whose vocabulary
is independent of the statutes by construction.
A paired experiment on 71~identical information needs isolates the
effect: with corpus, indices, systems, and relevance judgements held
fixed, rewording alone moves BM25's $\MRR{5}$ by $0.29$ against
dense's $0.09$ (Section~V-C), so leaky benchmarks manufacture a false
parity between lexical and semantic retrieval.
The methodology enables a controlled comparison of four first-stage
configurations on a 102-query benchmark over EU Climate Law: BM25
lexical search, a learned-sparse encoder (SPLADE), \nomic dense
retrieval (768-dim, 8{,}192-token context), and a hybrid combining
BM25 and \nomic via weighted Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF, $k{=}20$,
dense weight~5).
The corpus comprises 1{,}156 article-level provisions (1{,}166
retrieval units after splitting oversized amendment articles)
extracted from 72~EU legislative instruments via SPARQL and
Formex~XML; fusion parameters were tuned on a 49-query validation
split and the sealed 53-query test set was evaluated only once.
Under the controlled protocol, dense retrieval achieves
$\HR{5}{=}0.925$, substantially outperforming BM25
($\HR{5}{=}0.698$; Wilcoxon $p{=}0.004$).
SPLADE closes most of the gap to dense ($\HR{5}{=}0.887$,
$\MRR{5}{=}0.744$, $\NDCG{5}{=}0.717$) while carrying the highest
hard-negative rate of any system, so classical BM25 understates what
sparse retrieval can do here.
The hybrid achieves $\HR{5}{=}0.906$, $\MRR{5}{=}0.773$, and
$\NDCG{5}{=}0.721$, the highest MRR and NDCG of the three primary
systems, though SPLADE's point estimates are close behind and the
hybrid's improvement over dense alone is not significant
($p{=}0.614$); only 5 of 53 queries ($9.4\,\%$) are hard failures for
the hybrid.
An embedding-model ablation adds a null result: E5-large-v2 and
BGE-small-v1.5, both restricted to 512-token windows that truncate a
fifth of this corpus, show no significant difference from the
8{,}192-token encoder (Wilcoxon $p{\geq}0.055$), and a targeted check
finds the document-chunking strategy makes no measurable difference
either (Section~V-G).
The transferable finding is methodological: on a leakage-controlled
benchmark a strong general-domain dense retriever already performs
close to the best point estimate any system reaches on this corpus,
and weighted lexical-semantic fusion adds modest ranking gains
without a statistically significant coverage improvement, so we argue
leakage control and externally sourced information needs should be
standard practice when benchmarking statute-level retrieval.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Primary legislation presents significant challenges for legal
interpretation, particularly in identifying the relevant document,
which depends on precise wording, intertextual references, and
correctly identifying the applicable instrument among multiple
similarly titled sources.
These challenges are especially pronounced in EU climate and energy
law: the Emissions Trading System directive of 2003 has been amended
repeatedly, the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism borrows its
allowance definitions from that directive, and dozens of implementing
and delegated acts reuse the same vocabulary (\textit{free
allocation}, \textit{carbon leakage}, \textit{monitoring and
reporting}) for legally distinct obligations.
A retrieval system that returns a plausible but wrong instrument does
not produce an approximate answer; it produces the wrong law.
(Our evaluation, described in Section~IV, judges relevance at this
instrument level rather than the finer article level; Section~VII
discusses what that leaves open.)
The stakes are highest in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
pipelines, where the first-stage retriever determines whether
citation-grounded generation is possible at all~\cite{13}.
This paper evaluates that first stage.

The standard tension in legal retrieval is between lexical and
semantic matching, but which side of it wins is a claim about a
benchmark as much as about a system.
BM25 performs well on formulaic legal text, where exact term overlap
is a strong relevance signal~\cite{1}, but a compliance officer asking
whether her shipping company ``has to pay for the pollution its
vessels cause'' shares almost no vocabulary with ``surrender
requirements in respect of maritime transport activities''
(Directive~2023/959).
Dense retrievers~\cite{19} bridge that vocabulary gap, but bring
failure modes of their own: semantically similar passages from the
wrong instrument can rank highly because legal drafting recycles
structural boilerplate~\cite{11}, and off-the-shelf embedding models
often degrade on specialised corpora outside their training
distribution~\cite{13,18}.
Hybrid sparse--dense fusion is the commonly recommended
remedy~\cite{2,3,9}.
Whether that recommendation holds for statute-level retrieval over a
small, terminologically dense corpus is an empirical question, and
answering it fairly depends on a benchmark whose queries were not
written while consulting the statutes: when they are, they inherit
statutory wording, term overlap substitutes for relevance, and
lexical and hybrid systems are flattered by construction.
No prior hybrid legal-retrieval study controls for this.

Our first evaluation attempt showed this concretely.
In a pilot benchmark whose queries were written while consulting the
corpus, every configuration looked excellent, the hybrid appeared to
win outright, and the strongest system reached a perfect hit rate;
the queries had simply inherited the statutes' vocabulary, so the
benchmark rewarded term overlap rather than retrieval quality and
flattered BM25 in particular.
We therefore rebuilt the benchmark under an explicit leakage-control
protocol: every query was rewritten in the plain language of a
non-expert, and the test set was extended with queries derived from
written questions that Members of the European Parliament submitted
to the Commission, real information needs documented by their
reference numbers, whose official answers often name the very
instruments a correct retrieval should return.
Because those needs originate outside the corpus, their vocabulary is
independent of the statutes by construction rather than by rewriting.
To our knowledge no prior work on hybrid legal retrieval has
evaluated statute-level EU climate law, and none has controlled for
query--corpus vocabulary leakage this way; we argue the protocol,
plain-language rewriting plus externally sourced information needs,
should be standard practice for statute-level retrieval evaluation.

We ask three questions.
\textbf{RQ1.} Does hybrid sparse--dense retrieval improve
effectiveness and reliability over BM25-only and dense-only baselines
for EU Climate Law statute retrieval?
\textbf{RQ2.} What failure modes remain for the strongest
configuration, and what do they suggest about where retrieval effort
should go next? (This paper evaluates retrieval only; it does not
measure generation, so it cannot establish whether retrieval is the
bottleneck for a full RAG pipeline.)
\textbf{RQ3.} Does dense embedding-model choice on this corpus track
context-window length, or must it be benchmarked directly?

Our contributions are:
(i)~a leakage-control protocol for statute-level retrieval
evaluation, pairing plain-language rewriting of every query with a
test set extended by externally sourced information needs, plus a
paired experiment quantifying what uncontrolled leakage costs
(Section~V-C);
(ii)~an externally sourced query set: 31 of the 53 sealed test
queries derived from documented European Parliament written
questions, with vocabulary independent of the corpus by construction;
(iii)~a 102-query benchmark for statute-level EU Climate Law
retrieval with binary CELEX-level relevance judgements built under
that protocol, over a reproducible corpus of 1{,}156 article-level
provisions from 72~EU climate instruments extracted through a
documented two-phase SPARQL pipeline over CELLAR;
(iv)~a comparison of BM25, a learned-sparse encoder (SPLADE), a pure
dense bi-encoder, and weighted reciprocal rank fusion, with paired
significance tests (Wilcoxon, exact McNemar), bootstrap and
cluster-bootstrap confidence intervals, and per-query failure
analysis; and
(v)~an embedding-model ablation (E5-large-v2, BGE-small-v1.5) showing
that context-window length does not measurably predict retrieval
quality on this corpus, together with a chunking-strategy check and a
BM25 hyperparameter search run under the same validation/test
discipline as the RRF tuning.

To preview the results: on the leakage-controlled test set both the
dense retriever and the hybrid significantly outperform BM25 but are
statistically indistinguishable from each other, and the direction of
that comparison reversed as the test set grew, a caution against
reading small-sample differences between strong systems.
The residual failures are ranking errors rather than coverage gaps,
the largest a cross-instrument discrimination error that a different
open-weight encoder resolves from the same indexed text, pointing to
embedding-model choice rather than corpus preprocessing as the next
lever for improvement.

Section~II reviews related work; Section~III details the corpus,
retrievers, and fusion; Section~IV covers the benchmark and setup;
Section~V reports results; Section~VI discusses implications;
Section~VII concludes with limitations and future work.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Lexical vs.\ Dense Retrieval in Legal IR}

Mori et al.~\cite{1} find BM25 a competitive baseline for formulaic
legal text, where dense models underperform by glossing over legally
binding phrasing; Zheng et al.~\cite{7} find the opposite on
reasoning-intensive queries, and LRAGE~\cite{13} confirms
off-the-shelf dense retrievers underperform BM25 without domain
adaptation.
Li et al.~\cite{21} add that on large-scale Chinese legal case
retrieval BM25 consistently beats off-the-shelf dense models, so the
balance is corpus- and language-dependent; our results suggest it
also depends on how queries are phrased.

\subsection{Hybrid Retrieval}

Rayo et al.~\cite{3}, LegalRAG~\cite{9}, and HyPA-RAG~\cite{2} each
show hybrid fusion beating single-mode retrieval on regulatory or
multilingual legal text.
SPLADE~\cite{4} learns sparse representations that close the
vocabulary gap inside a single inverted index; we deploy classical
BM25 as the fusion partner for interpretability but add SPLADE as a
standalone baseline (Section~V-A) to check how much headroom that
leaves on the table.
Louis et al.~\cite{25} find domain-general legal models help fusion
zero-shot but can hurt once components are domain-trained unless
weights are re-tuned, consistent with our finding that an untuned
literature-default RRF configuration underperforms a corpus-specific
one; no legal-domain or EU-law-specific embedding model is evaluated
here (Section~VII).
Cormack et al.~\cite{14} established that rank-based fusion beats
score-based combination without score normalisation; Bruch et
al.~\cite{20} show analytically that RRF's parameters do not transfer
across collections, which our validation sweep reproduces on a much
smaller corpus.

\subsection{Retrieval Reliability and Legal RAG}

Reuter et al.~\cite{11} name Document-Level Retrieval Mismatch (DRM),
where shared structural boilerplate makes globally irrelevant
documents rank highly.
Ho et al.~\cite{12} argue doctrinal accuracy needs structural signals
beyond semantic similarity; CBR-RAG~\cite{5} and LexRAG~\cite{6} show
multi-turn legal grounding needs retrieval robust to topic drift.
Multi-stage embedding-plus-LLM-reranking works well in
COLIEE~\cite{10}, and LexDrafter~\cite{8} applies RAG directly to EU
legislative drafting, the closest prior work to our domain, though it
does not evaluate first-stage retrieval quality.

\section{Methodology}

The pipeline is a two-phase CELLAR extraction feeding dual BM25/dense
indexing, with both retrievers' outputs merged at query time by
weighted RRF; each stage is detailed in the subsections below.

\subsection{Corpus Construction}

We extract the corpus from the EU CELLAR repository in two phases.
A SPARQL query first retrieves every work URI associated with any of
21~EuroVoc climate and energy concept identifiers, which yields the
72~instruments; per-document manifestation queries then resolve the
Formex~XML (\texttt{fmx4}) download URL for each instrument, falling
back to the HTML manifestation for the few documents that lack one.
Formex is the Publications Office's structured drafting format, so
parsing it (with \texttt{lxml}) recovers the legal structure of each
act directly instead of reverse-engineering it from rendered HTML.

The retrieval unit is the legislative article, a deliberate choice:
EU articles are typically drafted to state one obligation,
definition, or procedure, so article-level units align with what a
legal user needs retrieved and avoid the arbitrary window sizes of
token-based chunking.
We have not measured how often an answer instead spans multiple
articles, annexes, recitals, or cross-referenced instruments, so this
is motivated by drafting convention rather than empirically verified,
and Section~V-G's chunking check speaks only to the five oversized
amendment articles, not this broader question.
Concretely, each \texttt{<ARTICLE>} element becomes one unit, with
text reconstructed from \texttt{<ALINEA>} leaf nodes to avoid
duplicated nesting; every record is validated against a schema, and
placeholder articles are discarded.
The exception is amending instruments, whose Article~1 can run to
tens of thousands of tokens because it contains every amendment the
act makes.
Five such articles exceed a 7{,}000-token limit (chosen to leave
headroom below the dense encoder's 8{,}192-token window) and are
split at the natural amendment-point boundaries; the resulting
sub-chunks inherit the parent \texttt{celex\_id}, so retrieval
results always resolve to a citable instrument.
Fig.~\ref{fig:tokhist} shows the resulting length distribution.

The final corpus contains \textbf{1{,}156} article-level provisions
(\textbf{1{,}166} retrieval units after splitting) from
\textbf{72}~CELEX instruments covering ETS, CBAM, Taxonomy,
LULUCF, F-Gas, Maritime MRV, ESR, the European Climate Law, Energy
Efficiency, CSDDD, and the Governance Regulation.
Instrument size is skewed, as is typical of EU legislation: a median
of 10~articles per instrument against a mean of 16.2, ranging from 2
(a short amending decision) to 72 (the European Green Bond Standards
Regulation), and 79.8\,\% of retrieval units come from Regulations
against 20.2\,\% from Directives.
The 21~EuroVoc identifiers and all 72~CELEX instrument IDs are
released with the code rather than listed here; we have not obtained
an independent legal expert's completeness assessment, so coverage
rests on the EuroVoc concepts alone.
Indexing extracts only \texttt{<ARTICLE>} elements; annexes, recitals,
tables, and footnotes are not indexed or retrievable.

\subsection{Sparse Retrieval - BM25}

The deployed lexical retriever is BM25Okapi~\cite{15}
(\texttt{rank-bm25}~0.2.2) with $k_1{=}1.5$ and $b{=}0.75$, checked
rather than assumed: the same validation grid search used for RRF
tuning prefers $k_1{=}1.8$--$2.0$, $b{=}1.0$, but that configuration's
hybrid has \emph{lower} test-set $\MRR{5}$ (0.737 vs.\ 0.773) and a
worse hard-negative rate (0.30 vs.\ 0.20), so we keep the defaults
rather than switch on an overfit signal.
Tokenisation preserves hyphenated terms of art (\textit{net-zero}) as
single tokens and applies neither stemming nor stopword removal, for
precision on differently scoped EU terms and fixed phrases
(\textit{free of charge}); this costs real performance
($+0.057$ $\HR{5}$, $+0.058$ $\MRR{5}$ available from stemming alone),
a trade we disclose rather than hide.
At query time BM25 scores all 1{,}166 units and returns the top~100.

\subsection{Learned-Sparse Retrieval - SPLADE}

To test whether classical BM25 understates what sparse retrieval can
do here, we add \texttt{naver/splade-cocondenser-ensembledistil} as a
standalone baseline (not fused into the hybrid).
SPLADE encodes both queries and documents into sparse vectors over
the tokenizer vocabulary via a masked-language-model head, term
weights are $\log(1{+}\mathrm{relu}(x))$-pooled over token positions,
giving implicit query expansion while remaining sparse and
interpretable.
At this corpus size (1{,}166 documents) we score by exact sparse dot
product rather than an inverted-index engine such as Anserini/Lucene.
The trade is deliberate: brute-force scoring carries no
approximate-retrieval error, so the SPLADE numbers below reflect the
model and not an index-pruning heuristic, but they equally say
nothing about learned-sparse behaviour, or its engineering overhead,
at the corpus scales where an inverted index becomes necessary.
Indexed on the same hierarchically chunked corpus as the other
retrievers, for comparability.

\subsection{Dense Retrieval - nomic-embed-text-v1.5}

The semantic retriever is \nomic~\cite{16}, a 768-dimensional
bi-encoder with an 8{,}192-token context window, loaded via
\texttt{sentence-transformers}.
Three properties drove the selection.
It is fully open-weight, which keeps the pipeline reproducible
without API access.
Its long context window embeds EU articles whole (only the five
oversized amendment articles described above need splitting), where a
conventional 512-token encoder would silently truncate roughly a
fifth of this corpus (Fig.~\ref{fig:tokhist}); Section~V-G tests how
much this actually matters.
Most importantly for the experimental design, it is a \emph{pure}
bi-encoder: models trained jointly for dense, sparse, and
late-interaction retrieval (such as BGE-M3) already encode lexical
signals in their dense vectors, which would muddy any conclusion
about what BM25 adds on top.

Following the model's asymmetric training scheme, queries are
prefixed with \texttt{"search\_query:"} and articles with
\texttt{"search\_document:"} before encoding.
Corpus vectors are L2-normalised and stored in a FAISS~\cite{17}
\texttt{IndexFlatIP}, where inner product on normalised vectors
equals cosine similarity; at 1{,}166 vectors, exact search is
trivially fast and no approximate index is needed.
At query time, FAISS returns the top~100 nearest neighbours.

\subsection{Reciprocal Rank Fusion}

Both retrievers run concurrently on each query
(\texttt{ThreadPool\-Executor}, two workers), and their ranked lists
are merged by weighted Reciprocal Rank Fusion~\cite{14}.
Each candidate document~$d$ receives the score

\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{RRF}(d) = \frac{w_s}{k + \mathrm{rank}_s(d)}
                  + \frac{w_d}{k + \mathrm{rank}_d(d)}
  \label{eq:rrf}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mathrm{rank}_s$ and $\mathrm{rank}_d$ are the
document's positions in the sparse and dense lists
($\infty$ if absent), and $w_s$, $w_d$ weight the two retrievers.
The constant $k$ is a positive smoothing term added to each rank in
the denominator; it sets how steeply a document's contribution decays
with rank position, so a small $k$ lets rank-1 results dominate the
fused score while a large $k$ flattens the differences between
neighbouring ranks.
The intuition of~\eqref{eq:rrf} is that a document ranked well by
both retrievers should beat a document ranked first by only one; the
weights let a systematically stronger retriever dominate ties.
The top~5 fused results form the system's output (and, in a full RAG
deployment, the context passed to generation).

We fuse ranks rather than raw scores because the two score scales are
incompatible: BM25 produces unbounded positive reals while cosine
similarity lies in $[-1,1]$, and any direct combination smuggles in
scale assumptions that rank-based fusion avoids
entirely~\cite{14}.
The operating point $k{=}20$, $w_s{=}1$, $w_d{=}5$ was chosen by grid
search on the 49-query validation split; the sealed 53-query test set
was evaluated only after these values were frozen.
Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep} shows the validation grid: ranking quality
improves as the dense retriever is up-weighted, degrades sharply at
large~$k$, and the deployed configuration is the highest-MRR cell
among those reaching $\HR{5}{=}1.000$ on validation; this
``highest-MRR-among-perfect-coverage'' selection rule was applied
after inspecting the grid rather than pre-registered beforehand, a
source of model-selection bias we disclose rather than correct for
post hoc, since the grid itself (Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep}) shows a broad
region of comparable cells rather than one selection-sensitive spike.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Benchmark}

The benchmark contains 102~queries covering all eleven EU Climate Law
instrument families, constructed in two stages.
Unlike broad multi-task legal benchmarks that aggregate hundreds of
tasks across jurisdictions~\cite{22}, it targets a single tightly
bounded corpus, which is what makes statute-level retrieval evaluation
and leakage control tractable.
An initial set of 71~queries was written in deliberately natural,
non-statutory language: an early pilot in which queries paraphrased
corpus wording produced near-perfect scores for all systems
($\HR{5}{=}1.0$), indicating query--corpus vocabulary leakage rather
than retrieval quality; all queries were therefore rewritten to
express the underlying information need without reusing statutory
vocabulary.
These 71~queries were split 70/30 by stratified difficulty (seed~42)
into a 49-query validation set (used exclusively for fusion-parameter
tuning) and a 22-query test set.
The paired leakage experiment of Section~V-C operates on exactly this
original 71-query set (49 validation plus 22 test), because only
these queries exist in both wordings: the corpus-derived phrasing of
the pilot and the plain-language rewrite.
The test set was subsequently expanded with 31~queries derived from
written questions submitted by Members of the European Parliament to
the Commission: each parliamentary question was screened for
answerability from legislation, paraphrased into plain language, and
mapped to its relevant CELEX instruments, with the parliamentary
reference number (e.g., E-002271/2024) recorded per query as an audit
trail.
In many cases the Commission's official answer cites the relevant
instruments, providing independent corroboration of the relevance
judgement.
Corpus membership was verified for every mapping before a query was
accepted, so no query requires an instrument absent from the corpus.
These 31~queries take no part in the paired leakage experiment, since
they never had a corpus-derived wording to be paired with; that is
also their value.
Because they originate outside the corpus, their vocabulary is
independent of the statutes by construction rather than by rewriting,
which makes them the strongest leakage control in the benchmark, and
each is traceable to a public parliamentary record.
Rewriting still lost something: re-running retrieval on 29 of these
31 queries' literal original text (2 reference pre-2024 terms not
retrievable via the current register) gives BM25 $+0.207$ $\HR{5}$
and $+0.271$ $\MRR{5}$ over the paraphrase, with dense essentially
flat, corroborating the paired leakage experiment of Section~V-C on
data it never touched.
Qrels were built by a single author, not pooled across systems; a
post-hoc pooling diagnostic (union of all five systems' top-10 hits)
finds candidates outside the recorded qrels for every query (mean
10.7), a possible annotation gap we quantify but a human would need
to resolve.
All reported results use the resulting sealed \textbf{53-query test
set}; the validation set was never evaluated against reported metrics.
To give a flavour of the derivation: parliamentary question
E-004153/2025 asks whether a ship calling at a mainland port en route
to an outermost region owes allowances for the first leg of the
voyage; this becomes the plain-language query \emph{``A ship sails
from the mainland, stops at another mainland port, then continues to
one of the EU's remote overseas regions --- is the whole journey
spared the carbon charge, or only the last leg?''}, mapped to
Directive~2023/959, whose derogation the Commission's answer cites.

Each query carries a difficulty tag used for stratification:
\emph{hard} queries share essentially no vocabulary with the statutory
text (e.g., ``the don't-make-anything-else-worse test'' for the
Taxonomy's \emph{do no significant harm} principle), while
\emph{standard} queries are naturally phrased but moderately
terminological.
Table~\ref{tab:benchmark} summarises the composition.
Relevance judgements target 62 of the 72 corpus instruments; the ten
never used as targets are annex-replacement amendment acts with no
self-contained content, excluded as relevance targets by
construction.

\subsection{Metrics}

We report three metrics at cut-off~5, matching the number of
provisions a downstream RAG stage would receive as context.
$\HR{5}$ measures coverage: the fraction of queries for which at
least one relevant instrument appears in the top~5 at all.
For grounded generation this is the make-or-break number: a miss
here cannot be repaired downstream.
$\MRR{5}$ measures how high the first relevant result lands, which
matters because generation quality degrades when the correct
provision sits below distractors.
$\NDCG{5}$ credits finding \emph{all} relevant instruments, not just
the first: 22 of the 53 test queries have two or more relevant
instruments (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}), and MRR is blind to
everything after the first hit.
Gains are binary; since retrieval is article-level, several articles
of one instrument can occupy the top~5, so truncation happens first
and deduplication second, crediting each relevant instrument once at
its highest-ranked occurrence (duplicate slots are not backfilled).

With only 53 test queries we quantify uncertainty several ways: 95\,\%
CIs for all three metrics by percentile bootstrap (5{,}000 resamples,
seed~42) and by a cluster bootstrap resampling target instruments
rather than queries (comparably wide on this benchmark); exact
McNemar tests for the binary $\HR{5}$ comparisons and Wilcoxon
signed-rank for $\MRR{5}$/$\NDCG{5}$ ($\alpha{=}0.05$, $n{=}53$),
with Cohen's~$d$, a matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation, and
Holm-Bonferroni correction across the six embedding-ablation tests
(none significant before or after).

\subsection{Hardware and Software}

All results were produced on consumer hardware, no GPUs or paid APIs:
primary systems on an AMD Ryzen~7 5700U laptop (CPU-only, Windows~11)
and the embedding ablation on an Apple~M1~Pro (CPU-only), with primary
numbers reproduced identically on both.
The software stack is Python~3.14.2 with
\texttt{sentence-transformers}~5.5.1, \texttt{faiss-cpu}~1.14.2,
\texttt{rank-bm25}~0.2.2, and \texttt{pydantic}~2.13.4, all pinned in
the repository; the combined indices occupy under 10\,MB.
All four dense/sparse encoders (nomic-v1.5, E5-large-v2,

given with each model in Section~III.

\section{Performance Evaluation}

\subsection{Overall Effectiveness}

Fig.~\ref{fig:effcomp} reports the headline comparison on the sealed
53-query test set: hybrid vs.\ BM25 $p{=}0.0005$; dense vs.\ BM25
$p{=}0.004$.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/effectiveness_comparison.pdf}
\caption{HR@5, MRR@5, NDCG@5 for all six systems, with 95\,\% bootstrap
  CIs. Primary systems (blue) and ablation baselines (orange) sit on
  either side of the dotted divider.}
\label{fig:effcomp}
\end{figure*}

Both semantic configurations beat BM25 by more than twenty points of
$\HR{5}$ (hybrid: $p{=}0.0005$, $d{=}0.579$; dense: $p{=}0.004$,
$d{=}0.433$): on a leakage-controlled benchmark, lexical retrieval
alone still misses roughly three queries in ten.
SPLADE, a standalone baseline rather than BM25's replacement, closes
most of that gap: its point estimates sit close to \nomic's and well
above BM25's, above the hybrid's on no metric, while carrying the
highest hard-negative rate of any system tested ($0.30$).

Between dense and the hybrid the Wilcoxon test finds no significant
difference ($p{=}0.614$, $d{=}0.120$): dense posts the best $\HR{5}$,
the hybrid the best $\MRR{5}$ and $\NDCG{5}$, and their confidence
intervals overlap substantially on every metric (Fig.~\ref{fig:effcomp}).
Neither reading is confirmed equivalence, only that this test, at this
sample size, detected no difference.
Part of the explanation is architectural: RRF fuses ranks and
discards the scores behind them, so it cannot weight the dense
retriever's confidence, and with dense already at rank~1 on 34 of 53
queries (Section~V-F) BM25's ranks are as likely to displace a
correct dense result as to confirm one (Section~V-E), consistent with
Bruch et al.~\cite{20}.
The honest answer to RQ1 is therefore split: fusion delivers a
significant improvement over the lexical baseline but no measurable
improvement over a strong dense retriever alone.

Dense retrieval recovers 13~queries that BM25 misses entirely,

Fusion is nearly lossless with respect to the dense ranking: the

The five split into four causes.
q080 is a cross-instrument discrimination failure: the relevant ETS2

(for q054, BM25 alone has the answer at rank~1).

by inspecting the indexed chunks, which matters because re-chunking
would not have helped.
Answering RQ2: the residual failures are concentrated in
encoder-specific discrimination and extreme vocabulary mismatch, not
in corpus coverage or the retrieval strategy.

\subsection{Ranking Quality}

Where does the hybrid's aggregate $\MRR{5}$ advantage ($+0.031$) come
from?
Restricting to the 48~queries both semantic systems hit, as such
analyses typically do, would exclude the 5~queries where dense and
hybrid disagree on coverage, precisely those most informative about
the $\HR{5}$ gap, so we report the full 53-query breakdown.
On coverage, both hit on 48, dense alone on 1 (q090, pushed just
outside the top~5 by fusion), hybrid alone on 0, and both miss 4.
On ranking, over the 48 queries where the comparison is defined, the
two place the first relevant result at the same rank on 31, the
hybrid higher on 8, and dense higher on 9.
The aggregate edge is one of magnitude: the hybrid more often
promotes a relevant instrument all the way to rank~1 (38~queries
versus 34 for dense and 23 for BM25), typically where BM25's
exact-term signal confirms an instrument \nomic placed just below a
semantically similar alternative.
This is a post hoc exploratory reading, not a pre-specified
mechanism; at this corpus size it moves the mean without reaching
significance.

\subsection{Embedding-Model Ablation}

\nomic's context window was part of its selection (Section~III), so
we tested how much that choice matters, re-running dense-only
evaluation with two alternative open-weight bi-encoders,
E5-large-v2~\cite{23} (335M params, 512-token window) and
BGE-small-v1.5~\cite{24} (33M params, same window), both indexed on
the 1{,}156 raw articles and both silently truncating the 21.1\,\%
of units exceeding 512 tokens (Fig.~\ref{fig:tokhist}); scores in
Fig.~\ref{fig:effcomp}.
Neither differs significantly from \nomic (six Wilcoxon comparisons,
$p{=}0.055$--$1.0$).
E5-large-v2 posts the best MRR/NDCG point estimates of any system
($\MRR{5}{=}0.806$, $p{=}0.216$; $\NDCG{5}{=}0.794$, $p{=}0.055$) on
a different query subset than \nomic (49/53 agreement).
BGE-small-v1.5, a fraction of the parameter count, posts the best
coverage ($\HR{5}{=}0.943$, $p{=}0.564$), retrieving q080, which all
three primary systems miss, from exactly the same indexed text
(Section~V-E).
Two readings, kept apart: context-window length does not predict
retrieval quality here, models truncating a fifth of the corpus are
statistically indistinguishable from one reading it whole, so the
truncation argument for model selection carries less weight than
assumed at design time; but these are null results at $n{=}53$,
failing to detect a difference is not evidence of equivalence, and we
claim parity only in that weak sense.
A targeted check isolates chunking from encoder choice: re-indexing
\nomic with the five oversized Article~1 instruments left whole
(truncated at encoding time, not split) reproduces $\HR{5}$, $\MRR{5}$,
and $\NDCG{5}$ to four decimal places across all 15 affected test
queries, including q080, direct evidence, not just chunk inspection,
that chunking is not the cause of that failure. This is one
alternative on the five affected instruments, not the full
article/paragraph/amendment-point comparison a complete sensitivity
analysis would run.
Answering RQ3: neither context-window length nor parameter count
predicts which model posts the best point estimate on any metric
here, so embedding-model choice on a corpus like this has to be
benchmarked directly rather than read off the specification sheet.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{Why does fusion not pay off here?}
Fusion improves on its stronger component only if the weaker one
rescues queries the stronger one misses, and that almost never
happens here: dense misses only four queries and BM25 recovers just
one.
With so little unique lexical signal to add, fusion can at best
re-rank what dense already found, while the dense-favouring weight
needed to stop BM25's errors from corrupting the ranking also
suppresses that one genuine save.
This is not a general argument against hybrid retrieval: reported
gains typically come where the dense component is weaker or the
corpus is large enough that retrievers diverge more often; on a
small, terminologically dense statutory corpus a strong bi-encoder
leaves BM25 too little room to help.

\textbf{Small samples mislead.}
The hybrid-vs-dense direction reversed as the test set grew (dense
led on MRR at $n{=}22$; hybrid leads at $n{=}53$) while staying
non-significant at both sizes, so ``fusion hurts'' and ``fusion
helps'' were both noise, including in our own earlier drafts.
Legal IR benchmarks in this size range are common, and comparisons
drawn from them without significance testing deserve the same
suspicion.

\textbf{Evaluation design decides the apparent winner.}
The sharpest result came before any system comparison: rewording the
queries alone, with everything else fixed, moved BM25 far more than
dense (Table~\ref{tab:leakage}), and the independent
raw-parliamentary-question check (Section~IV-A) reproduces the
pattern on untouched data.
Annotators who write queries while reading the corpus reuse its
vocabulary, so we suspect this inflation recurs in other
specialised-domain benchmarks and systematically flatters lexical and
hybrid systems; externally sourced information needs are a cheap,
auditable defence.

\textbf{Practical implications.}
These results support a simple recipe: start with a strong open-weight
bi-encoder, treat BM25 fusion as low-cost insurance (negligible
overhead, and on this evidence negligible benefit), and tune it if
used.
The literature default ($k{=}60$, equal weights)~\cite{14} degrades
validation $\HR{5}$/$\MRR{5}$ from $1.000$/$0.810$ to $0.898$/$0.679$
because with only $\sim$1{,}200 candidates BM25's errors displace
dense rank-1 results, consistent with Bruch et al.~\cite{20}; a
post-hoc full-grid sweep (Fig.~\ref{fig:heatmap}), run to characterise
sensitivity rather than re-select parameters, reproduces the deployed
numbers exactly and shows $\HR{5}$ plateauing at the dense-only
ceiling (0.925) from $w_d{=}8$, a broad ridge rather than a narrow
spike.

\centering

Finally, the failure analysis locates the remaining headroom in
encoder choice rather than corpus engineering: the largest residual
failure is a cross-instrument discrimination error that another
open-weight encoder resolves from the same indexed text, and no
failure traces to chunking or coverage.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper's most transferable lesson concerns benchmark
construction rather than architecture.
The sharpest single result is not a system comparison at all: with
corpus, indices, systems, and relevance judgements held fixed,
rewording the queries alone shifts $\MRR{5}$ by $0.29$ for BM25 but
only $0.09$ for the dense retriever (Table~\ref{tab:leakage}), enough
to turn an apparent three-point lexical--semantic parity into a
twenty-five-point gap, and the independent raw-parliamentary-question
check (Section~IV-A) reproduces the pattern on data that experiment
never touched.
Annotators who write queries while reading the corpus inherit its
vocabulary, so we expect this inflation in other specialised-domain
benchmarks, where it systematically flatters lexical and hybrid
systems; plain-language rewriting and externally sourced information
needs are a cheap, auditable defence that we argue should be standard
practice when benchmarking statute-level retrieval.
We set out to test whether hybrid sparse--dense fusion improves
statute-level retrieval for EU Climate Law, and found that answering
the question fairly required building the evaluation as carefully as
the system: a reproducible 1{,}156-provision corpus of 72~EU climate
instruments, a 102-query benchmark under an explicit leakage-control
protocol with 31 test queries from documented European Parliament
written questions, and a comparison of BM25, SPLADE, a pure dense
bi-encoder, and weighted reciprocal rank fusion with significance
testing throughout.

The findings are easy to state, with the caveats attached to each.
Both dense-only and hybrid beat BM25 by wide, significant margins,
and SPLADE closes most (not all) of that gap while carrying the
highest hard-negative rate tested.
Between dense and the hybrid no test detects a difference; fusion is
a cheap safety net, not a necessary ingredient, and we characterise
the deployed system as dense-dominated weighted RRF ($w_d{=}5w_s$),
not a balanced hybrid.
The embedding ablation shows no significant difference between
512-token and long-context encoders, and a targeted re-index shows no
measurable effect from chunking on the five instruments it touches,
so encoder identity, not context length or chunking, carries the
remaining variance.
We also closed a symmetry gap in our own tuning: grid-searched BM25
hyperparameters improve on validation but don't generalise to the
test set, so we kept the defaults rather than switch on an overfit
signal, while stemming alone would have bought $+0.057$ $\HR{5}$ left
on the table for exact-match precision.
The remaining failures are encoder-specific discrimination and
register-gap errors rather than coverage or chunking problems, so
embedding-model selection, not corpus preprocessing, is a more
promising next step for practitioners than it is a proven one: this
rests on one demonstrated case (q080) and an aggregate parity result,
not a measured comparison of remediation strategies.

Several limitations bound these conclusions.
The test set, at 53~queries, yields wide confidence intervals, and a
null result at this size cannot exclude a small true effect.
Relevance is judged at the instrument (CELEX) level rather than the
article level, which is lenient toward systems that find the right
act but the wrong provision.
Query paraphrases and relevance mappings were produced with
generative-AI assistance and verified by the authors.
A completed check by a second annotator (Faisal Ali Agha, LL.M.,
Trinity College Dublin, energy arbitration law) on the 14-query
overlap subset found moderate agreement (Cohen's $\kappa{=}0.42$,
95\,\% CI $[0.20,0.62]$): below the $0.60$ publication-quality bar
but well above chance, indicating relevance judgements are broadly,
if imperfectly, consistent across annotators without undermining
the relative comparisons between systems, which use one qrel set
throughout.
31 of the 53 test queries (58.5\,\%) still carry independent
corroboration via the Commission's official answers to the source
parliamentary questions.
Finally, the study covers one legal domain and one language, and all
three dense models (plus SPLADE) are general-domain: no legal-domain
or multilingual EU-law-specific encoder was evaluated, so
``dense is enough'' is a claim about strong general-domain
bi-encoders on small statutory corpora, not about dense retrieval in
general.
\nomic's selection is also not pre-registered: version control shows
it replaced two earlier candidates, each swapped for a stated
technical reason before the sealed test set reached its current size,
evidence against cherry-picking but not a pre-registered protocol.

Future work follows from these limitations: extend relevance
judgements from the instrument to the article level; broaden the
completed second-annotator check (Cohen's $\kappa{=}0.42$ on
14~queries) to the full test set and reconcile disagreements toward
the $0.60$ bar; address the cross-instrument discrimination failure at
the encoder level via fine-tuning or re-ranking, and compare weighted
RRF against a cross-encoder reranker over the same first-stage
candidates; scale the test set via the European Parliament's open
data interface, which our query-derivation protocol can largely
automate; and evaluate end-to-end RAG generation by feeding the top-5
retrieved passages to an LLM and scoring its answers against the
Commission's official written replies to the 31~EP-derived queries.
